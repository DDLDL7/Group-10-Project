# National Electricity Grid Network Analysis — Report

## 1. Dataset description

The dataset is synthetic, generated by a seeded script (`generate_data.py`,
`random.seed(42)`) so every run is byte-for-byte reproducible. It is grounded
in Ghana's real grid operators (ECG, NEDCo, GRIDCo, VRA) and the real West
African Power Pool (WAPP), which interconnects Ghana with Côte d'Ivoire,
Togo, Benin, Burkina Faso, and Guinea. **All coordinates, capacities,
commissioning years, and connections are illustrative** — they must not be
presented as verified measurements of Ghana's actual electricity
infrastructure.

The generator produces three related tables:

| Table | Rows | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `utilities.csv` | 10 | Utility ownership records (ECG, NEDCo, GRIDCo, VRA, plus regional cross-border utilities) |
| `substations.csv` | 44 | 36 substations across 10 Ghanaian regions + 8 cross-border interconnection/hub substations |
| `lines.csv` | 55 | Transmission/distribution lines connecting substations, including intra-region mesh links, inter-regional GRIDCo backbone lines, and WAPP cross-border interconnections |

## 2. Cleaning steps and data quality

`src/cleaning.py` coerces numeric columns (`Latitude`, `Longitude`, `Voltage
(kV)`, `Capacity (MVA)`, `Commissioning Year`, `Length (km)`), drops
exact-duplicate rows, validates that every substation's coordinates fall
within a West African bounding box, and validates that every `Source`/
`Destination Substation ID` and `Utility ID` referenced in `lines.csv`
actually exists in `substations.csv`/`utilities.csv`.

Running this against the generated dataset found **zero** missing values,
zero coercion failures, zero duplicate rows, zero out-of-bounds coordinates,
and zero broken references — expected, since the generator is deterministic
and internally consistent. This does not mean the cleaning step is
unnecessary: it is the same pipeline that would catch these issues in a
messier, real-world asset register, and the tests in `tests/test_cleaning.py`
verify it actually does (using deliberately-broken sample data) rather than
relying on the generated dataset happening to be clean.

## 3. Exploratory data analysis findings

**Substations by region**: Greater Accra has the most (6), followed by
Ashanti (5); Western, Central, Eastern, and Volta each have 4; the eight
cross-border hub/interconnection substations are spread one-per-country
(Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Togo, Benin, Guinea) plus dedicated border
stations.

**Voltage distribution**: 161 kV (11 substations) and 330 kV (10) —
transmission-tier — together make up nearly half the network; 69 kV (8,
Bulk Supply Points), 11 kV (9) and 33 kV (6) make up the rest.

**Substation status**: 43 of 44 substations are Active; 1 Inactive.

**Line status**: 53 of 55 lines Active; 2 Under Maintenance.

**Most-connected substations** (by line count): Mallam, Kumasi Central, and
Cape Coast tie for the most connections (5 each), followed by Achimota,
Tema, Legon, Takoradi, Koforidua, Ho, and Bolgatanga (4 each) — a mix of
regional capital-area hubs and cross-border-adjacent stations.

**Utility footprint (by line count)**: GRIDCo (utility ID 3) operates the
most lines (24, consistent with its role as the transmission-tier operator
carrying inter-regional and cross-border backbone links), followed by NEDCo
(14) and ECG (10); the remaining utilities each operate a handful of
cross-border interconnection lines.

**Isolated substations**: two substations appear in `substations.csv` but
are never referenced as a source or destination in `lines.csv` — Savelugu
Substation (Northern region, likely left unconnected by the generator's
random 55% intra-region linking probability) and the Conakry Transmission
Hub (Guinea), which the generator's `border_links` list never actually
connects to anything. This is a genuine structural gap in the generated
data, not a bug in our analysis code, and it directly shapes the network
results in Section 4.

## 4. Network analysis (NetworkX)

The grid is modelled as an **undirected** graph — substations as nodes,
lines as edges — since AC power can flow either direction along a line
depending on system conditions.

- **Degree centrality**: Cape Coast, Mallam, and Kumasi Central tie for the
  highest degree centrality (0.116 each — 5 connections apiece out of 43
  possible), matching the raw line-count ranking from Section 3.
- **Betweenness centrality**: Cape Coast Substation again ranks highest
  (0.525), followed by Takoradi (0.516), Kumasi Central (0.501), Koforidua
  (0.498), and Ho (0.436) — these are the substations that sit on the most
  shortest paths between other pairs of substations, i.e. the inter-regional
  bridge points, distinct from the purely local hubs.
- **PageRank**: Bolgatanga Substation (Upper East) ranks highest (0.047)
  despite modest degree centrality — it earns its importance from being
  connected to other well-connected substations (the Burkina Faso border
  interconnection), which PageRank rewards but simple degree centrality
  does not.
- **Connected components**: the base graph is **not** a single component —
  it has 3 components (sizes 42, 1, 1). The two singletons are Savelugu
  Substation and the Conakry Transmission Hub, both structurally isolated
  in the generated data (see Section 3). The main 42-node component is
  otherwise fully connected.

### N-1 contingency check

The highest-degree substation (Cape Coast) was removed from a copy of the
graph. Result: connected components went from **3 (sizes 42, 1, 1) to 5
(sizes 20, 18, 3, 1, 1)** — the network **does fragment**. Removing Cape
Coast splits the main 42-substation component into three pieces (20, 18, and
3 substations), meaning Cape Coast is a genuine structural cut point in this
particular generated network, not merely a locally well-connected hub.

This is a more decisive resilience finding than the illustrative example in
the assignment brief (which reported no fragmentation on a differently-built
graph that silently excluded unconnected substations). Building the graph
strictly per the technical specification — every substation in
`substations.csv` becomes a node, whether or not it has any lines — surfaces
both the pre-existing isolated substations and a genuine single point of
failure, which is a more complete and honest picture of network resilience.

This remains a simplified, graph-based educational approximation of the
contingency analysis grid operators run before scheduling maintenance — it
is **not** a substitute for a real power-flow, protection-coordination, or
security-constrained study. Graph centrality does not represent electrical
load, voltage stability, or protection behaviour, so "Cape Coast is a cut
point" should be read as a structural observation, not a claim that a real
outage there would strand those substations electrically.

## 5. Geographic and business-intelligence analysis

`src/geo.py` renders a standalone Folium map (`outputs/substation_map.html`)
with substations colored by voltage tier and lines drawn as polylines,
filterable by voltage-tier layer.

`src/bi.py` computes four **explicit proxy** metrics, since the dataset has
no real load, population, or demand data to compute genuine operational
indicators:

- **Utility footprint by region** — line counts per utility per source
  region, a proxy for infrastructure footprint.
- **Capacity flags** — substations in the bottom/top quartile of capacity
  for their voltage tier, a proxy for upgrade candidates vs. over-provisioned
  assets (real utilization would require actual load data).
- **Underserved regions** — regions with both substation count and total
  capacity below the cross-region median, a proxy for growth opportunity
  (real assessment would require population/demand data).
- **Asset age profile** — `Commissioning Year` bucketed into decades,
  cross-tabbed with region and status, as a proxy for where older,
  higher-fault-risk infrastructure is concentrated.

## 6. Limitations of using synthetic data

- All figures are generated by a seeded script for reproducibility across
  the team, not measured from any real utility. They must not be cited as
  facts about Ghana's actual grid.
- Graph centrality metrics are structural proxies for "importance" — they do
  not model electrical load flow, voltage stability, or protection
  coordination, and should not be read as definitive reliability findings.
- The business-intelligence metrics (capacity flags, underserved regions,
  asset age) are proxies built entirely from the synthetic capacity/region/
  year fields; a real assessment would require actual demand, population,
  and outage-history data this dataset does not contain.
- The N-1 contingency check is a simplified graph-connectivity test, not a
  full power-flow or security-constrained contingency study.

## 7. Next steps / future improvements

- Investigate whether adding a redundant link to/through Cape Coast
  Substation (the identified N-1 cut point) would restore full connectivity
  after a single-substation loss — a natural next question raised by the
  contingency finding in Section 4.
- Consider whether the generator's `border_links` list should connect the
  Conakry Transmission Hub to something, and whether Savelugu's isolation is
  an acceptable outcome of the random intra-region linking or worth a
  minimum-degree guarantee per substation.
- Feed the cleaned `substations.csv` into GridCare-Lite so outages can only
  be logged against real substation IDs (per the integration point described
  in `Project_Plain_Guide.md`).
- If real load/demand data ever became available, replace the proxy BI
  metrics in `src/bi.py` with genuine utilization and reliability measures.
